01 · The condition

What is sleep apnea?

Sleep apnea is a disorder in which breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep. Pauses can last from a few seconds to minutes and may happen hundreds of times a night — fragmenting sleep and starving the body of oxygen. There are two main types.

OSA

Obstructive sleep apnea

The most common form — the upper airway physically collapses or is blocked, restricting oxygen even as the body keeps trying to breathe.

CSA

Central sleep apnea

Less common and harder to spot — the part of the brain that controls breathing misses a beat, so the body simply doesn't try to breathe.

02 · The device

What is a CPAP machine?

CPAP — Continuous Positive Airway Pressure — delivers a steady stream of pressurized air through a mask worn over the nose or mouth, splinting the airway open through the night. Pressure and timing are tuned to the patient, and the machine quietly logs every breath it delivers.

03 · The stakes

And what is “compliance”?

Compliance is how consistently you use the machine as prescribed. Insurers define it with a hard threshold — and it's where most people fall off, often without realizing the clock is running.

User Interviews

Six CPAP users, one recurring wish.

I sat down with six people who actually wear the mask every night — an engineer, a veteran, a self-treating researcher, a designer, a retired lawyer, and a long-haul trucker. Very different lives, strikingly similar frustrations.

What I heard, again and again

Mask fit is the real battle

Nearly everyone cycled through many masks (Kathy tried nine) before one stuck — and discomfort is exactly where people quit.

Apps are all-or-nothing

myAir felt too shallow; OSCAR too complex. Everyone wanted the missing middle — real detail, without the overwhelm.

They want a guide, not a dashboard

From “virtual assistant” to ChatGPT consults, users already reach for AI to interpret the data and say what to try next.

It feels clinical — it should feel human

Compliance pressure makes therapy feel like being a medical device. People wanted warmth, autonomy, and encouragement.

02 The Problem

Most people quit CPAP therapy before it can help them.

CPAP apps swing between two failures: they bury patients under raw charts, or they hide the data entirely behind a single green checkmark. Neither builds the confidence and curiosity that keep someone wearing an uncomfortable mask night after night — so most stop within the first month.

The Opportunity

“How might we make CPAP data feel actionable and human — so people feel guided, not graded?”

The Solution

Distill each night into one story — what happened, what likely caused it, and the next thing to try — wrapped in calm charts and wearable context.

Competitive Analysis

Powerful but cold — or polished but elsewhere.

I scanned the apps people already use: the CPAP-specific tools built for the data, and the consumer wellness apps built for the habit. The gap between the two — clinical depth with a human voice — is where Nocta lives.

03 Brand & Design System

A night sky, not a hospital.

Most CPAP tools look like the machine they came from: grey, clinical, scored in red and green. Nocta's brand starts from the moment people actually open it: half-awake, in the dark, a little worried about a number. The name, the logo, the sky, the coach and the voice all work toward one thing: lowering that worry.

04 Features

Eight surfaces, one calm story.

01

Onboarding that earns the first morning

New CPAP users arrive anxious and short on patience. Setup only asks what changes how Nocta reads a night (goals, sleeping position, other conditions, mask, insurance window), then sets expectations before night one.

  • 01 One question per screen, with a step counter
  • 02 Every step skippable, and each says why it's asking
  • 03 A preview of tomorrow's insight, using example data
  • 04 Nox introduces itself, including what it won't do

02

The why horizon

Instead of a wall of charts, the first thing you see is a verdict and the shape of your night: a few words on what happened, one line with a reference point, and a curve of events per hour against your usual.

Tap the info icon and Nox shows its work: the exact numbers it reasoned from, whether the night fits a pattern it's watching, and how confident it is.

03

The full night, one chart at a time

The detail layer: events by type on the night's real clock, sleep stages from your watch, and the machine's four signals (flow, pressure, leak and snore).

Tap any chart for its own page, like Apple Health: the key number, the graph at full size, and a plain-language “About.”

04

A 20-second morning check-in

How you felt and what happened that day is half of what Nox learns from, so the check-in sits right under tonight's suggestion. The first question is answerable in place: tap “Tired” and the rest opens with it already picked.

Four questions, with back and skip on every step. Once it's logged, it folds down to a single slim line.

05

Ask Nox

Nox opens already knowing what you were looking at. From the horizon it starts with last night's numbers and offers three follow-ups; from Trends or a chart, it starts there instead.

Its limits are part of the design. Ask it to change your pressure and it says that's your doctor's call, then offers to prepare a summary for them.

06

Trends over time

Zoom out to 7, 30 or 90 nights. Four tiles (AHI, leak, usage and pressure) double as tabs for one large chart, each with its change right beside the number.

Week-level insights, a best-versus-worst night comparison and journal patterns connect what you logged to how you slept.

07

Therapy & compliance

A compliance outlook says plainly whether you can still clear your insurer's bar, and by which night. It's framed as a path forward, never a grade.

Machine settings, your mask and every part's replacement date fold into one line until something is due. One tap exports a clean, AI-free summary for your doctor.

08

Connected devices

Apple Watch, Oura, Fitbit, Garmin, Samsung and Whoop add heart rate, HRV and sleep stages to the same night as your therapy data.

Connecting runs a full mock OAuth flow: choose what Nocta can read, watch it pull in your nights, and switch any signal off later without disconnecting.

05 AI Safety Rails

An insight that knows its limits.

The moment an app interprets clinical data, it risks becoming a regulated medical device. Nocta's AI lives inside hard rails: every insight is typed JSON with cited data, a confidence label, and a disclaimer — and certain patterns route straight to “talk to your doctor.”

Honest, not cheerful

No “great job!”, no streak trophies. A bad night is named calmly and plainly.

Hedged on cause

“This pattern often suggests…” — never “this means” or “you have.”

Never diagnoses

It won't name a condition or recommend a pressure change. Ever.

Escalates to a doctor

Central-apnea or SpO₂ patterns trip a physician-referral flag.

07 Impact & Measurement

What it sets out to change, and how I'd know.

Reduce confusion

by translating raw CPAP data into personalized, plain-language insights people can understand and act on.

Build confidence

by helping users see what's working, what's not, and the one adjustment worth trying tonight.

Support the long haul

through gentle, honest habit-building that keeps people on therapy past the month that usually breaks them.

How I'd measure it

Nocta hasn't launched, so I won't claim results. These are the metrics I set out in my capstone to judge it by, plus one guardrail the AI makes necessary.

Activation

Share of new users who finish onboarding, complete a first check-in and open their first insight.

North star · Compliance

Share of users who reach 4+ hours on 70% of nights within 30 days, compared with people using only their machine's app.

Engagement

Which surfaces earn repeat use (check-ins, Ask Nox, doctor exports), and which don't.

Guardrail · Safety

Zero insights that suggest a pressure change or name a condition, and how often a “talk to your doctor” flag leads to a visit.

With a team, next I would

  1. Have a sleep physician review the AI's language and escalation rules before any real user sees an insight.
  2. Run a 30-night diary study with new CPAP users, to see whether the morning insight actually changes behavior.
  3. Test the why horizon with users 50 to 75, the core audience, for legibility and trust at a glance.
  4. Work with engineering on the SleepHQ and OpenAI integrations, and the privacy model for health data.
