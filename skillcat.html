SkillCat is a skilled-trades training platform that helps people learn trades like HVAC and earn the certifications that get them hired, and it helps employers train their teams. I worked across SkillCat's product as a product designer, contributing to both the consumer-facing app and the B2B side that companies use to manage training.

My work spanned three connected surfaces: the mobile and desktop redesign of the learner experience, a new admin dashboard for employers, and the smaller craft details that make the product feel rewarding to use. Alongside the design, I wrote the specs that translated these decisions into something engineering could build.

01 · The Starting Point

Six screens from the pre-redesign SkillCat app — a deep catalog, but no clear path to a credential.

SkillCat had a deep library of trade courses and a lot of screens, but the experience was catalog-first: you landed in a wall of categories and certificates and had to assemble your own route to the credential that actually gets you hired. The value was all there — it just wasn't obvious how to reach it.

What wasn't working

  • 01

    Catalog-first — learners landed in categories (EPA 608, Trade School Diplomas…) with no guided path.

  • 02

    The credential that gets you hired was hard to find among everything else on offer.

  • 03

    Little sense of progress or "what's next" — easy to enroll, easy to drift.

  • 04

    A five-tab nav (Social, Skill Check, Class, Simulations, Profile) split attention away from the goal.

02 · Research & The Landscape

Trade learners are outcome-driven — and the competition chasing them runs from Duolingo to trade schools.

Trade learners are outcome-driven: they're studying HVAC, electrical, or plumbing to earn a certification and a better paycheck — often mid-career, often learning on the job (OJT). I started by getting the vocabulary right (OJT, EPA 608, HVAC) so the product could speak the trade instead of edtech jargon.

01

Outcome-driven

They're here for a credential and a raise, not points and streaks for their own sake.

02

Field language

OJT, EPA 608, HVAC — the product should sound like the worksite, not a classroom.

03

Mixed confidence

First-timers and seasoned techs both use it — the path has to work for both ends.

I audited both direct trade-education players and the broader learning apps people already know — pulling apart their typography, color, and tone to find an opening SkillCat could own.

Direct — trade schools & cert providers (Interplay Learning, Penn Foster, HVACR School…): credible but corporate and dated.

Indirect — consumer learning apps (Coursera, Skillshare, Duolingo…): polished and habit-forming, but mostly light and soft.

What the scan told me

  • Duolingo is playful and habit-forming, but too cartoony to feel credible for older trades users.
  • Airbnb uses modern 3D illustration sparingly — warmth without noise, a model worth borrowing.
  • Trade-school brands skew corporate and dated — a clear opening for something modern and trustworthy.
  • Most edtech is light and gentle; the trades read as practical and high-contrast — a chance to look different and earned.

03 · Finding The Brand

Three brand directions explored — the dark navy + safety-orange system won.

I explored a few directions — a bright, playful take; a light, corporate one; and a darker, more industrial system. The bright direction felt too casual for the audience, and the corporate one blended in with the competitors. The dark, high-contrast navy + safety-orange system felt practical, credible, and ownable — closer to the gear and worksites the audience already trusts.

04 · Iterating The Experience

Five key screens, three rounds each — V1 → V3 across the learner journey.

Onboarding moved from a form-first signup toward a few guided questions that quietly build a personalized path. The home experience shifted from a flat catalog to a clear "path to a paycheck" with an obvious first step. Each round cut friction and made the goal — and the next action — harder to miss.

Before · V1

Form-first signup

  • A flat catalog of categories to pick from blindly
  • No personalization, no sense of a destination

After · Shipped

Portfolio

Course view

Inside a course — the lesson list, your progress, and the next thing to do. The hardest balance: show everything without losing the next click.

"You Passed!"

05 · Testing & Insights

An unmoderated Maze study — 7 participants, 3 flows, and one clear weak point.

I ran an unmoderated Maze study with 7 participants across three core flows — Create Account, Start Course, and Edit Path — capturing success, mis-clicks, time on task, and self-rated understanding of where the product was taking them. Starting a course landed cleanly. Editing the path was where it broke down.

Per-flow results — tap a flow to see its heatmaps

Insights

  • The tree-map navigation isn't intuitive yet — confusion about where to start, or how to preview a path.
  • Most users clicked the nav bar or home course cards before finding the path module.
  • Mobile dropped users off at "Edit Path" — freezing and reloading on path flows. A Maze prototype flaw, but it cost real frustration.
  • Some users tapped path cards before discovering swipe-to-remove.
  • What's not working: preview navigation, Edit Path functionality, "swipe to delete."

Potential fixes

  • Add path education on first arrival — a short walkthrough of the tree map.
  • Replace swipe-to-remove with an explicit delete icon on path cards.
  • Increase the CTA strength of the first course module so the starting point is unmissable.

06 · The Learner Redesign

Five flows that carry a learner from adding a course to earning an EPA 608 certification.

Enrolling is the easy part, the value a learner actually came for is the credential at the end. The hard part is carrying someone all the way from picking a course, through lessons and hands-on tasks, into a proctored final exam, and out the other side holding a real, hireable EPA 608 certification, all without losing them at any step.

I assisted in designing the end-to-end learning journey as one continuous path: add a course to your plan, work through its lessons and tasks, then sit the EPA 608 Universal final exam — proctoring and identity checks included — and receive the certification. Every step is a complete set of states (in progress, complete, pass, fail, review), so progress always feels earned and the next action is never in doubt. 07 · The Admin Dashboard

The B2B admin dashboard, feature by feature — Inbox, People, OJT review, and the plan builder.

SkillCat isn't only used by individuals, employers use it to train their teams. Admins needed to assign training courses to multiple employees and track completion. The existing flow treated everything as flat, one-off selections, which broke down against how training actually works in the real world.

I assited in designing the employer side as a full dashboard for running training across a team. Assigning courses was reframed from a flat list of selections into building a structured, sequenced plan: select employees, stack courses in the right order, and set a due date on each. Around that sit the tools admins work in day to day; a role-aware Inbox that unifies every pending approval and action, on-the-job review for grading hands-on task submissions, and a people-management view that scales well past a handful of employees. Below, the dashboard broken into its core features.

The dashboard, feature by feature

A role-aware, unified task system — every approval, assignment, and action an admin owns, surfaced in one place.

Pick employees, stack courses into a sequenced plan with a due date on each row, then review and assign — the three-step plan builder in action.

Review and grade the photos learners submit for hands-on (OTJ) tasks — approve, score, or send it back for another attempt.

Search, filter, and manage the whole team — roles, progress, and bulk actions that scale well past a handful of employees.

08 · Reflection

Working across SkillCat's product — consumer onboarding, B2B tooling, and specs — meant constantly shifting altitude: from the feel of a single animation frame, up to a multi-step enterprise workflow, and back down into the written rules that make it real. The throughline was the same everywhere: understand the actual job the person is trying to do, then shape the product around that rather than around the existing layout.

What this case study shows about how I work

  • I reframe problems at the model level before touching layout — the admin dashboard is the clearest example.
  • I design complete state systems, which is what makes my work clean to build and quick to ship.
  • I care about craft and emotion, down to easing curves, because in a learning product those details drive whether people come back.
  • I can carry work from first concept all the way to engineering handoff.
